# Lovepop

On May 11, 2015, less than three weeks before graduation from Harvard Business School (HBS), Wombi Rose and John Wise sat in a Toyota Tundra pickup truck behind Boston's South Station. Their company, Lovepop, sold three-dimensional paper art cards at a small retail kiosk. After 16 months of work, bootstrapping with savings and a few loans, the founders had received seed funding offers from two different investors. They had to decide which offer to accept.

Rose and Wise had founded Lovepop during their first year at HBS after discovering the 3D paper art of kirigami on a school trip to Vietnam. Soon after, they began creating their own pop-up cards using computer design techniques they had learned while earning undergraduate degrees in naval architecture. Since founding the company in February 2014, they had worked on Lovepop between classes and on weekends. Both planned to work full-time on scaling the company after graduation.

Sixteen months after founding, Lovepop had achieved $209,000 in cumulative revenues, but was not yet cash-flow positive and funds were very low. A $40,000 loan from OnDeck Capital would last through graduation, but with a monthly burn rate of roughly $40,000, they needed more capital.

The first offer was from Techstars Boston, a startup accelerator that would provide three months of mentoring, networking, and investor exposure. In return for that support and $18,000 in cash, Techstars wanted 6% equity. Techstars also indicated that Lovepop was eligible for a $100,000 convertible note and that it would help the founders secure additional funds before an institutional round.

The second offer was from the California incubator Founder.org, which had offered a $300,000 convertible note. Both investors wanted an answer by the next day.

## Founding Lovepop

Rose and Wise met in 2005 as engineering undergraduates at New York's Webb Institute. The school offered only one degree, naval architecture and marine engineering, and every student received a full-tuition scholarship. Students spent time in shipyards, learned welding and steel work, and sailed on ships as part of their training.

After earning degrees in naval architecture and marine engineering in 2009, Wise took a job designing ships in Louisiana. After four years designing patrol boats for the U.S. Coast Guard and Navy, he decided to apply to business school in the hope of someday building his own company. Rose enrolled at MIT for a master's degree in Computation for Design and Optimization and then joined McKinsey & Company, working mainly on oil, gas, and shipping-related projects.

In early 2013, Wise called Rose to say he had been accepted to HBS. Rose recalled that Wise had realized his life was consumed by work and that he had no ownership in what he was building. Rose applied in the second round and was accepted in March 2013. They began HBS with the thought that they would start a business together.

In September 2013, they moved into an apartment in Harvard Square and began the MBA program. They researched several entrepreneurial opportunities, including an industrial fish farm and natural gas trucking and trading, but rejected them after evaluating the business models and their own knowledge gaps. Rose also worked on several startups during his first year at HBS, including BevSpot, which sold analytics software to bars, and an app for cataloging ship inspections.

In January 2014, Rose and Wise traveled to Vietnam for HBS's Field Immersion Experiences for Leadership Development (FIELD). One evening in Ho Chi Minh City, they found a stall selling pop-up cards. It was their first encounter with kirigami, a form of origami involving cutting and folding paper into small sculptures. The aesthetics were striking, and the underlying design techniques reminded them of naval architecture. They realized they could engineer almost anything into a pop-up card.

Before leaving Vietnam, Wise and Rose found a manufacturer, packed cards into their suitcase, and planned to sell them to HBS classmates. They initially priced the cards at $5, then raised prices to $8 and then $13 as they learned customer willingness to pay. Within 30 days, they sold 150 cards.

The early sales convinced them to start a business. They concluded that they needed to design the cards themselves, own the art, and tailor the products to the American market. During spring break of their first year, they designed a holiday line. They bought a Silhouette electronic cutting machine, experimented with design software, and used AutoCAD to design the first card.

The first original Lovepop card was a paper replica of the mid-19th century clipper ship *The Young America*, whose architect, William H. Webb, had founded Webb Institute. After designing six cards this way, they moved to a laser cutter at Danger!Awesome, a makerspace in Cambridge's Central Square. The design process began with a paper sketch, then a software rendering, then programming the cutting machine or laser cutter, and finally assembling the cut paper by hand using glue and string. Each design required multiple prototypes.

## Launching the Product

As summer 2014 approached, the partners considered how to launch. They could sell on marketplaces such as Etsy or Amazon, sell through their own website, sell wholesale to boutiques, or sell directly to consumers offline through a Lovepop shop, craft fairs, or retail kiosks. Amazon and Etsy offered broad exposure but involved fees, intense competition, and limited customer ownership. Wholesale relationships were appealing but uncertain and potentially thin-margin. Offline direct-to-consumer sales seemed more feasible through craft fairs or small retail kiosks.

The founders decided to start by selling directly to consumers online and at local craft markets. On April 1, 2014, Lovepopcards.com launched with cards in nine categories, including birthday, love, and wedding. Cards sold for $8 to $15. Unlike traditional greeting cards, Lovepop cards were blank inside because the founders believed personal messages were more meaningful than printed greetings.

The night before launch, Rose and Wise emailed their extended network to generate demand. The first day produced almost $2,000 in sales on the site. The second day fell to $50.

They also began a content marketing campaign through the Lovepop blog. The first blog post, "Gratitude Guide: How to Write Meaningful Thank You Cards," offered advice on writing thoughtful thank-you notes and argued that handwritten gratitude remained appropriate and expected even as communication became more digital.

In April 2014, the founders began selling cards at the SoWa Open Market in Boston's South End. The market drew local artisans, farmers, food trucks, and brewers. There, the founders saw customer response directly. Customers had not seen anything like the cards before, and the founders interpreted the reaction as evidence that Lovepop was not only selling a product but helping people connect.

## Early HBS Support and Founder.org

By spring 2014, the company was taking shape, but every milestone brought new questions. The founders sought advice through HBS resources. In mid-April 2014, they entered HBS's New Venture Competition (NVC), a pitch contest for early-stage businesses, and were selected as semifinalists. Wise later described NVC as an early catalyst because judging feedback, semifinalist status, and summer incubation opportunities helped them understand the opportunity and motivated them to continue.

As NVC semifinalists, the founders became eligible for an interview with Founder.org, a California-based incubator and investor founded in 2012 by Michael Baum. Founder.org supported young companies founded by full-time students across North America and Europe. Unlike a traditional incubator, it did not offer office space; instead, it offered a year-long remote mentorship program through videoconferencing. Each week, Founder.org mentors met with founders from the 50 companies in the cohort on Google Hangout.

In June 2014, Founder.org accepted Lovepop into its second cohort. The program did not involve cash for equity; it provided guidance. In July 2014, Wise and Rose traveled to Paris for a conference with the cohort. They learned Founder.org's eight dimensions of company-building: idea, team, product, business planning, market development, customer development, operations, and fundraising.

## Summer 2014

During the summer between their two years at HBS, Rose and Wise focused heavily on Lovepop. They received a 12-week summer fellowship from HBS's Rock Center, including a weekly stipend of $600 for 12 weeks to offset living expenses. They also used the HBS i-lab as company headquarters.

Rose spent Fridays, Saturdays, and Sundays selling cards at Boston-area open-air markets. He loaded merchandise and a tablecloth into Wise's truck, drove to a local market, unloaded inventory, guarded his gear, sold cards for 10 to 12 hours, then returned home to count cash and inventory. The work was exhausting, but the revenue was significant and the fair cost only about $100.

Wise spent the summer vetting manufacturers in Vietnam. By then, Lovepop sold 12 original designs, including six holiday cards. In addition to the Lovepop website, the team listed products on Etsy and filled custom card orders. They also signed a wholesale contract with The Grommet, an online retailer specializing in handmade and unique gifts, to produce a set of six holiday cards.

Lovepop applied to appear on *Shark Tank*, a U.S. reality television show in which entrepreneurs pitched to investors. With only $6,000 in sales, the odds were long, and after a few interviews the founders were told they had not been selected.

As fall 2014 approached, Rose and Wise debated whether to return to HBS for their second year. The business consumed time after class and on weekends, leaving little time for academics, networking, or social life. Wise initially advocated deferring the second year, partly because he had planned to use HBS recruiting to pivot from engineering. Ultimately, both returned, partly out of concern that they might never complete the degree otherwise.

At one point, they considered shutting down the business. Instead of expanding aggressively into mall kiosks, they chose to focus in the short term on online sales, assuming that online sales would require less effort. By the end of August 2014, Lovepop revenues totaled nearly $40,000.

That fall, the founders prepared for the holiday season. In October, they launched a Kickstarter campaign to fund production of Christmas and other holiday cards. Within three weeks, 454 people pledged more than $21,000, more than double the original goal. By the end of 2014, Lovepop had generated $95,000 in revenues and incurred roughly $18,000 in operating losses.

## Manufacturing and Kiosks

The founders were pleased with 2014 progress but saw many areas for improvement. Lovepop's procurement cycle had four major steps: card design, prototyping, placing an order with the manufacturer, and delivery about six weeks later. Lovepop paid 50% of the shipment cost upfront and 50% on delivery. Quality was often poor. Shipments arrived with wrong colors or inconsistent quality, and the founders were concerned about labor conditions and reliability.

In January 2015, Wise traveled to Vietnam to investigate. Factory visits convinced him that manufacturing could be brought in-house. He believed that doing so would lower costs, raise product quality, improve delivery speed, and give Lovepop more control over working conditions.

In March 2015, Wise met Ha Trinh Quoc Bao, a kirigami designer who had designed a few cards for Lovepop. Wise asked whether Bao could set up a Lovepop factory. Within two months, Lovepop moved production from contract manufacturers to a small factory in Da Nang, Vietnam. Rose and Wise gave Bao $15,000 to open the factory. It employed 10 people, measured 80 square meters, had two laser cutters, and had production capacity of 100 cards per day.

While Wise worked on manufacturing, Rose explored offline retail beyond open-air markets. Boston's South Station train terminal offered Lovepop a one-month pop-up shop around Valentine's Day. Rose asked Colin Spillane, an employee and fellow Webb alumnus, to build and manage the kiosk. Spillane built the $2,000 kiosk with Home Depot plywood in his garage. During February 2015, Lovepop sold $34,000 of cards through the kiosk in one month. For fiscal year 2014 and the first four months of 2015, the kiosk channel was the largest sales channel, suggesting a potentially profitable channel that might scale nationally.

In spring 2015, Lovepop enrolled in HBS's 10-week Venture Incubation Program (VIP), which helped Harvard students launch and scale companies through peer and expert mentoring, networking, workshops, and events. Through VIP, the founders received mentorship from Adrian Gill, an HBS graduate and former vice president of Puma's footwear division. Gill advised them on go-to-market strategy and creative direction.

The founders were also selected for HBS's Rock Accelerator, which awarded them $5,500 toward business expenses. The team spent the stipend on inventory.

## Greeting Cards in 2015

In 2015, the greeting card publishing and related products market had estimated wholesale sales of $4.8 billion. Single greeting cards accounted for 55% of sales and boxed greeting card sets for 12.4%. Lovepop estimated total retail revenues for the industry at about $7 billion.

Analysts considered the industry to be declining at about 5% per year, due partly to e-card competition and consolidation in downstream markets such as bookstores. The market was highly concentrated. Hallmark Cards Inc., whose products were sold in more than 30,000 U.S. stores, had 61.9% market share. American Greetings Corporation (AGC), owner of the high-end Papyrus brand, had 22.9%.

Greeting card publishers sold through wholesale and direct-to-consumer channels. Wholesale accounted for most revenue. Walmart alone accounted for 14% of industry revenue in 2015, and Target accounted for 5%. Direct-to-consumer sales accounted for 11%. Hallmark and AGC sold much of their inventory through big-box, drug, and grocery stores. Lovepop's founders believed this distribution strategy created resistance to innovation because manufacturing gross margins were low, around 25%, and manufacturers bore inventory risk at retail customers.

Despite the rise of digital communication, U.S. consumers purchased 6.5 billion cards in 2014. Consumer needs had shifted toward broader product variety and more segmented offerings. In 2012, greeting cards retailed for an average of $2 to $4, while Papyrus and other high-end cards could cost up to $10. About 80% of greeting card buyers were women.

Wedding invitations represented another large stationery opportunity. Digital invitations were common, but couples who chose paper invitations often paid a substantial amount. In 2014, the average wedding budget allocated $439 for invitations, and the average guest list size in 2015 was 136.

## Financing Lovepop

Lovepop's first funding came from the founders. Rose and Wise each contributed $15,000 in savings during the first year to build the website, purchase inventory, and buy equipment. As the company grew, they continued to invest money and sought external funding.

In August 2014, Rose and Wise won a $10,000 convertible grant in a Founder.org pitch contest. It would automatically convert into equity if the company raised a financing round of at least $500,000. In October 2014, Dorm Room Fund Boston offered a $20,000 convertible note. Rough Draft Ventures, the student-led division of General Catalyst, invested $12,500. A friend, Josh Rothman, loaned the company $30,000. In early spring 2015, the Rock Accelerator awarded the company a $5,500 grant for inventory.

By April 2015, Lovepop was running out of money. With help from HBS classmate Sean Salas, then working on Camino Financial, the team secured a late-April loan from OnDeck Capital for $40,000 at 28% annual interest. By the end of April 2015, Lovepop had cumulative sales of $209,000. The loan would last through HBS graduation but not much further.

Lovepop's monthly operating expenses in April were around $64,000, and the team expected costs to rise as it hired additional employees and covered kiosk start-up costs. Kiosk start-up costs were estimated at roughly $20,000 per kiosk. The plan was to open two outlet-mall kiosks near Las Vegas, three in California, and one kiosk in Washington, D.C.'s Union Station.

## Techstars

During their final semester at HBS, Julia Austin, a technology entrepreneur and HBS visiting professor, saw a Lovepop card and offered to introduce the founders to Ty Danco, managing director of Techstars Boston. One company had dropped out of Techstars' 2015 summer cohort, and Danco encouraged Lovepop to apply.

Lovepop was not a traditional technology company, and there was internal debate at Techstars about fit. But Techstars was impressed by the team and product. Techstars particularly liked the founders' response to the application question, "What's your biggest accomplishment?" Instead of prose, the team sent a video about a project in which they and friends built a small steamboat-style raft for viewing the July 4 fireworks on Boston's Charles River. Danco interpreted the video as evidence of technical skill, imagination, daring, and entrepreneurial mentality.

On April 28, 2015, Techstars offered Lovepop a spot in its summer cohort. The terms were the same as for the other 11 companies: $18,000 in cash in exchange for 6% equity, plus an optional $100,000 convertible note.

## Incubators and Accelerators

Startup incubators emerged in the United States in the late 1950s and became prominent during the technology boom of the 1990s. They generally offered office space and administrative support to new ventures for a low fee and sometimes provided investor introductions. Many were not-for-profit, and many had ties to higher education. After the dot-com bubble burst in 2001, many incubators shut down.

In the mid-2000s, a new type of startup support organization emerged: the seed accelerator. Y Combinator was established in 2005 in Silicon Valley as a boot-camp-style program offering training and support in exchange for equity. Accelerators were fixed-term, cohort-based programs that exposed founders to mentors and ended in a public pitch event or demo day.

Accelerators typically enrolled entrepreneurs for 10 to 12 weeks, provided office space and a small amount of seed funding, and attempted to compress learning and progress. In 2014, there were an estimated 215 accelerators in the United States, up from 28 in 2009. The top selling point was exposure to investors. Techstars was founded in 2006 in Boulder, Colorado, and expanded to Boston by 2007. By 2014, it had 14 active programs globally. Among 483 Techstars graduates in 2014, 77% were still operational and 11% had been acquired. In 2015 U.S. accelerator rankings, Techstars placed among the top programs.

## Comparing the Offers

As HBS graduation approached, the founders felt the stakes rising. Despite progress, they were running out of cash. In April 2015, Techstars Boston was finalizing its summer cohort and aggressively courted Lovepop. On April 25, the founders received a video call from Techstars while at dinner. Techstars promised to help Lovepop close a $300,000 seed round before the accelerator program even started.

Meanwhile, the founders were finishing their Founder.org remote mentorship program. Founder.org also offered seed funding: a $300,000 convertible note at a $3 million valuation cap. Founder.org wanted this to be an exclusive seed round, forcing the team to choose between the two offers.

Friends gave conflicting advice. Wise's girlfriend encouraged them to join Techstars, believing they would be stubborn to turn it down. A trusted classmate and experienced investor, Niko Hrdy, believed the Founder.org note was founder-friendly and reasonable and advised taking the money.

Rose leaned toward Founder.org because it was much less dilutive than Techstars. The founders had not yet given up equity, and Rose thought they should preserve ownership while scaling kiosks. Wise leaned toward Techstars because of its name recognition and promise to help raise additional funding.

There was also a small possibility that Lovepop might appear on *Shark Tank*. In late April, Rose received an email from a *Shark Tank* executive requesting updated sales figures. The odds were low: in 2014, less than 1% of 45,000 applicants received a taped meeting with the "sharks," and even fewer appeared on the show. But airtime could produce major sales gains.

During the first week of May, Rose and Wise calculated the financial implications of each offer. They asked whether it made sense to give away 6% so early, whether Techstars' brand and network added enough value, whether HBS, Founder.org, the i-lab, and the Rock Accelerator already provided enough support, and whether Techstars could truly help them raise $300,000 for a paper-card company.

With no clear decision, they left the pickup truck and headed across the Fort Point Channel to Boston's Seaport District to continue the discussion over a beer.
